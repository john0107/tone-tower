# Jyutping guide: research and editorial notes

Updated 9 November 2026.

The guide introduces the notation with a worked syllable, then teaches initials, finals and tones. The initials section covers all 19 initials with short explanations and inline recordings, giving more space to sounds that need it. English comparisons are explicitly identified; articulation instructions and recordings remain the reference for the actual sounds. Three collapsed questions after the six tones cover the traditional nine-tone count, Mandarin tone relationships and everyday pronunciation, in that order.

## Editorial approach

Each explanation should answer a practical question: what sound does a spelling represent, what should the learner listen for, or why does a pronunciation differ? Use concrete examples and state the relevant condition. Omit generic study advice that adds no explanation. The guide moves directly from the worked syllable into the sounds; it has no separate “How to use this guide” section or abstract language-experience disclosure.

Use the same label-and-explanation rows for initial pairs and j/ng as for the finals, in one continuous list without extra subsection headings. Keep play controls compact and unboxed so they fit into the text. Explain tone notation through a concrete example such as fu1/fuk1; omit introductory sentences that merely announce the topics to follow.

Keep audio beside the sound it illustrates. Cover the seven initials familiar to many English speakers briefly, and give j, ng, the aspiration pairs and gw/kw enough explanation to be useful. Explain aspiration and the paper test immediately before the initial-pair rows; a second disclosure and a large table would repeat that material.

The final reader review explains vowel-free nasal syllables once in the Finals m/ng row: ng is the whole final in ng5, but the initial in ngaa4. It also separates historical tone correspondences from changes within a word and distinguishes voicing from aspiration. Avoid restating the examples as generic study advice.

## Language experience

- [Flege and Bohn (2021), The Revised Speech Learning Model](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108886901.002) proposes that existing sound categories, perceived differences and the quantity and quality of input influence new sound-category learning. This supports a general account of why learners have different starting points, not a prediction of an individual’s difficulty.
- [Balas (2017), The influence of second language vowels on foreign language vowel perception](https://doi.org/10.3765/plsa.v2i0.4085) studied Dutch vowel categorisation by Polish learners with different second-language experience. Experience with rounded front vowels affected perception. This is evidence about prior language experience beyond a first-language label; it is not a Cantonese study or a basis for ranking language families.
- [Mok, Leung, Lan and Yu (2019), The Acquisition of Cantonese Vowel Length Contrast and Vowel Rounding Contrast by South Asian Students in Hong Kong](https://ling.cuhk.edu.hk/people/peggy/ICPhS2019_MokLeungLanYu.pdf) found differences between discrimination and production and effects of phonetic environment. It supports treating hearing and producing a contrast as related but distinct tasks.

This background informs the examples and the distinction between recognising and producing sounds. It does not appear as a separate reader-facing note, and it is not a basis for ranking nationalities or language families by learning difficulty.

## Spelling and pronunciation

- [LSHK’s official scheme](https://lshk.org/jyutping-scheme/): origin in 1993, initials and finals, syllabic nasals, six baseline tone digits. Tone digits remain on the same line as the letters.
- [Open Cantonese initial pairs](https://opencantonese.org/books/cantonese-life-1/pronunciation-guide/pronunciation-lesson-1/3-initial-pairs): five aspiration pairs, including z/c and gw/kw, and a paper test for the puff of air. Explain the test in one or two sentences beside the playable pairs: hold paper near the mouth and compare an unaspirated initial with its aspirated partner; the latter should move it more. This tests aspiration, not vocal-fold vibration. No separate aspiration dropdown is needed.
- [Open Cantonese’s yu lesson](https://opencantonese.org/books/cantonese-life-1/pronunciation-guide/pronunciation-lesson-4/6-the-final-yu): tongue and lip position; yu is one vowel without an added initial glide.
- [Jyutping.org’s English guide](https://jyutping.org/en/learn/): vowel contrasts, initial ng and unreleased stop endings. Comparisons with English are aids, not universal sound definitions.
- [EdUHK’s tone explanation](https://corpus.eduhk.hk/cantonese/eng/chapter3/3-0.html): traditional entering-tone categories and why Jyutping uses six tone numbers.

### Why this guide uses Jyutping

Keep the Yale context brief and near the introduction. Jyutping is the LSHK spelling scheme used for this guide’s recordings and typed answers; its Latin letters and tone digits work with ordinary keyboards. Some books and courses use Yale, which represents the same Cantonese sounds with different spelling and tone notation. Explain this reason for the guide’s choice without teaching a second romanization system. Label the spelling system when linking a source that uses Yale.

### Why do i and u sound different in some finals?

[Open Cantonese’s -ng lesson](https://opencantonese.org/books/cantonese-life-1/pronunciation-guide/pronunciation-lesson-4/10-the-finals-ending-with-ng) and [-k lesson](https://opencantonese.org/books/cantonese-life-1/pronunciation-guide/pronunciation-lesson-5/8-the-finals-ending-with-k) explain that i in ing/ik and u in ung/uk differ from the vowels said alone, with a slightly more open mouth. Their tables transcribe these vowels as [ɪ] and [ʊ], compared with the longer [iː] and [uː] in open finals. The optional finals answer describes them as slightly more open and shorter. The inline playable pairs use credited recordings actually present in the catalog: si1 (詩) and sing1 (升) illustrate i; fu1 (夫) and fuk1 (幅) illustrate u. Display the characters belonging to those recordings rather than substituting a homophone. Keep the explanation focused on vowel quality and length; the entering-tone count belongs in its own answer after the tones.

## Questions after the tones

### Why do some resources say Cantonese has nine tones?

Define entering or checked syllables (入聲) as syllables ending in -p, -t or -k. The closure stops the sound without a final burst. Explain the traditional count as six non-stop-final tone categories plus three stop-final pitch categories, giving nine. Jyutping uses six digits because the stop-final categories share the high, middle and low pitch levels written as 1, 3 and 6.

The audible comparison table uses available recordings: fu1 夫 / fuk1 幅 for high pitch, fu3 副 / baat3 八 for middle pitch, and fu6 父 / sap6 十 for low pitch. These illustrate pitch categories; they are not minimal pairs or a matched recording experiment. Do not label the fuk1 recording as the homophone 福 or add unavailable fok3/fuk6 examples. State the practical pattern plainly: stop-final syllables normally use tone 1, 3 or 6. Add the changed-tone qualification that dip2 is a changed reading of 碟, whose basic reading is dip6. [CUHK’s Cantonese Grammar audio examples, chapter 1](https://www.cuhk.edu.hk/lin/cbrc/CantoneseGrammar/multimedia/01.htm) demonstrate stop-final changed readings using Yale romanization. A stop-final rising tone is therefore not automatically invalid.

### How are Mandarin and Cantonese tones related?

- [Jyutping.org’s guide for Pinyin readers](https://jyutping.org/cmn/learn/) provides tone diagrams and comparisons. Cantonese tone 1 and Mandarin tone 1 are broadly similar in pitch shape, as are their high-rising tone 2 categories. This is a comparison of pronunciation, not a rule for predicting the tone of a shared character.
- [Xu, Li and Mok (2014), The Effect of Orthography on L2 Perception](https://ling.cuhk.edu.hk/people/peggy/XuLiMok_TAL2014.pdf) distinguishes phonetic similarities from statistical relationships between tones in shared-character readings. Its tables give both directions of correspondence, with different percentages. The study concerns Cantonese listeners learning Mandarin; it is not a direct evaluation of a Mandarin-to-Cantonese learning method.
- [Michelle Fullwood’s Cantonese–Mandarin sound-correspondence tables](https://michellefullwood.com/languages/cantonese/correspondences/) compare initials, finals, tones and complete syllables using Unihan character readings. Place this link after the compact Mandarin-to-Cantonese tone table. Explain its value: a known Mandarin reading can suggest likely Cantonese sounds, and the percentages show how frequently a correspondence occurs in that dataset. Describe what the resource helps readers infer and how reliable the patterns are, rather than giving instructions for its controls.
- [EdUHK’s six-tone lesson](https://corpus.eduhk.hk/cantonese/eng/chapter3/3-1.html) describes tone 2 rising towards high pitch and tone 5 rising from low towards middle pitch. Inline fu2/fu5 buttons beside the explanation make the distinction audible: listen for how high the rise ends, not just whether a tone rises. This is listening advice, separate from guessing a shared character’s reading.
- [EdUHK’s Mandarin-third-tone examples](https://corpus.eduhk.hk/cantonese/scramble/tones/s3.html) illustrate both Cantonese 2 and 5, including sau2 in 手 and mou5 in 母. [Leung, A Study of the Cantonese Hearsay Particle wo from a Phonetic Perspective, manuscript pages 7–8](https://www.lib.eduhk.hk/pure-data/pub/201710378.pdf) supplies background on historical initial voicing and tone splits. Use this material only to explain the relevant historical relationship; the page does not need a separate history question.

Keep the pronunciation comparison and historical character relationships together in this one answer, with their distinction made explicit. The compact table runs from Mandarin to Cantonese: Mandarin tone 1 often corresponds to Cantonese 1; Mandarin 2 to Cantonese 4; Mandarin 3 to Cantonese 2 or 5; Mandarin 4 to Cantonese 3 or 6. Its examples have playable recordings: 天 tiān/tin1, 牙 yá/ngaa4, 好 hǎo/hou2 and 馬 mǎ/maa5, 四 sì/sei3 and 父 fù/fu6. The two Mandarin-third-tone examples show why that reading does not determine a single Cantonese tone. These are broad tendencies and memory aids; checked syllables, alternate readings and changed tones limit prediction.

The same answer introduces the historical upper or yin register (陰, tones 1/2/3) and lower or yang register (陽, tones 4/5/6), associated broadly with earlier voiceless and voiced initials. Define voicing through vocal-fold vibration: a sustained mmm vibrates, while sss does not. This is distinct from the airflow contrast called aspiration. History helps explain why m/n can offer a memory clue for some Mandarin-third-tone characters: 馬 mǎ/maa5 and 你 nǐ/nei5 use Cantonese 5, while 好 hǎo/hou2 and 手 shǒu/sau2 use Cantonese 2. Modern initials do not determine the tone, and exceptions are not limited to one character. [CUHK’s nau2 entry](https://humanum.arts.cuhk.edu.hk/Lexis/lexi-can/pho-rel.php?s1=n&s2=au&s3=2) includes 扭 niǔ/nau2 as one example; do not imply that this is the sole exception. The examples carry the explanation without a generic closing instruction about remembering readings or learning pitch.

Do not reproduce a probability table without a specific dataset and direction of prediction. Conditional probabilities cannot be reversed, and historical correspondence does not imply that the tones sound alike. The compact table omits percentages; the linked exploration tool provides more detailed, dataset-specific tables for interested readers.

### Why might everyday speech sound different from the dictionary?

- [Open Cantonese’s introductory pronunciation page](https://opencantonese.org/books/cantonese-life-1/unit-0/5-cantonese-pronunciation) describes common Hong Kong variation, including n/l and the loss or addition of initial ng. The guide uses nei5/lei5 and ngo5/o5 as recognition examples, without describing speakers’ ordinary pronunciation as laziness.
- [Cantowords’ 頭 entry](https://cantowords.com/dictionary/%E9%A0%AD), reading 4, gives the tau2 reading in tool names such as 斧頭 fu2 tau2. The guide compares 頭 tau4 (head) with the second syllable of 斧頭 fu2 tau2 (axe), using existing recordings from the same speaker: commons-88201954 and commons-149277076. Both examples are playable.

Distinguish variation between speakers from a word’s changed-tone reading within the same answer. Explain the selected example directly: 頭 is tau4 when it means “head”; in 斧頭 fu2 tau2, the same character is tau2. Compare the second syllable of the compound recording with the single-character recording. Omit generic advice to “learn the whole word” and unexplained comparisons with Mandarin tone-change rules. Keep tone-related detail within these three questions: further historical terminology, character families, grammar and particle equivalences, and loanword lists are outside this beginner overview. The inline aspiration tip and vowel-final question stay beside their relevant sound sections, without broader cross-language comparison boxes.
